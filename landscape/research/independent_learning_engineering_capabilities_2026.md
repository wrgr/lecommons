# Independent exploratory map of learning-engineering work and capabilities

**Research snapshot:** 26 August 2026  
**Status:** Independent discovery draft for validation; not a systematic or scoping review and not a statement of field-wide consensus.

## 1. Executive synthesis

This investigation used the supplied working boundary, rather than an IEEE competency model, as its search boundary. No IEEE or ICICLE competency framework was searched, retrieved, or used as an ontology. The resulting map treats learning engineering as a **cycle of consequential decisions**: frame a learning problem; model the intended capability; design aligned experiences and measures; build and instrument a usable system; interpret evidence; test and revise; implement in an organization; and govern effects over time.

### What the evidence supports most strongly

Across design-based research, assessment, learning analytics, improvement/implementation science, accessibility, and evaluation sources, competent work repeatedly requires the ability to:

1. **Frame the problem with the people and system affected**, separating a learning problem from participation, motivation, access, workflow, or policy problems.
2. **Make an explicit learning-and-measurement argument** connecting a target capability to tasks, observations, interpretations, and decisions.
3. **Design coherent practice, feedback, and assessment**, using learning evidence without treating a general research finding as a context-free recipe.
4. **Build observable and reliable interventions**, including instrumentation, data-quality checks, versioning, and traceable design rationales.
5. **Evaluate both effects and mechanisms**, including null, heterogeneous, and harmful effects—not merely engagement, completion, or statistical significance.
6. **Improve iteratively without confusing local optimization with causal proof**, and preserve enough documentation to learn across cycles.
7. **Design for implementation, adaptation, and sustainment**, because an efficacious artifact can fail through poor fit, weak capacity, or fidelity/adaptation trade-offs.
8. **Exercise data, accessibility, equity, and AI stewardship throughout the lifecycle**, rather than adding a compliance review after deployment.

The recurring unit is usually a **team capability**, not a mythical universal “learning engineer.” Common-core competence is best understood as the ability to perform some decisions and to recognize, brief, interrogate, and collaborate with specialists on others. Psychometric modeling, causal inference, production software/data engineering, security/privacy engineering, and algorithmic audits have a particularly strong claim to specialist status.

### What adjacent fields made visible

Explicit learning-engineering accounts foreground iterative evidence use. Adjacent fields reveal less visible failure modes: construct underrepresentation and invalid score use (assessment); missingness and proxy error (data science); local workflow and organizational readiness (implementation science); adaptation versus fidelity (implementation research); interaction and accessibility barriers (HCI/WCAG); surveillance, power, and contestability (learning-analytics ethics and AI governance); and the distinction between a working product, adoption, learning, and sustainment. These are not automatically individual competencies; several are obligations or distributed controls.

### Provisional or disputed findings

Evidence does **not** establish that every practitioner must code, conduct psychometrics, run experiments, or train models. Nor does it establish that rapid A/B testing is generally appropriate in education: short-run metrics can miss transfer, retention, opportunity costs, subgroup harms, and instructional mechanisms. “Fidelity” is also contested where local adaptation is necessary. Universal Design for Learning is influential design guidance, but the breadth and quality of outcome evidence for the framework as a whole remain debated; this report therefore grounds accessibility primarily in observable barrier-removal and WCAG conformance, not allegiance to a branded method.

## 2. Exploration record

### Search passes and systems consulted

This was a bounded, exploratory web-and-literature run. Searches were performed through the OpenAlex Works API and direct web retrieval of DOI landing pages and authoritative sites (IES/NCEE and What Works Clearinghouse, NIST, W3C, AERA/APA/NCME Testing Standards, Carnegie Foundation, CAST, and publisher or open-access journal pages). The general web-search connector returned an authorization error, so discovery relied on direct sites, known-title/DOI checking, OpenAlex metadata, and backward/forward conceptual chaining. This limitation matters: it likely reduced retrieval of recent practitioner presentations, RFPs, portfolios, and live job postings.

Principal query families were:

- **Field explicit:** `"learning engineering" process`, team, evidence, improvement, job, graduate outcomes.
- **Work function:** problem diagnosis; learning theory to design; evidence-centered design; practice/feedback; instrumentation and data quality; design-based research; causal evaluation; implementation, adaptation, scaling, sustainment.
- **Adjacent capability:** assessment validity/fairness; learning-analytics ethics/privacy; HCI and accessibility; software/data lifecycle; improvement and implementation outcomes; AI risk management.
- **Failure/counter-search:** educational software null effects; engagement without learning; construct validity failure; algorithmic bias; implementation failure; fidelity versus adaptation; UDL evidence critique; heterogeneous treatment effects.
- **Professional work:** sampled graduate outcomes and occupational/framework pages. Because durable authentic postings were sparse and volatile, employer evidence was not used to elevate any candidate to “core.”

### Coverage, limitations, and stopping judgment

Sources cover K–12, higher education, workplace/product contexts, assessment, learning analytics, public-sector guidance, and health-derived implementation science with an explicit transfer boundary. Peer-reviewed conceptual papers, empirical evaluations, synthesis/standards, government reports, and professional guidance are represented. English-language and North American/European sources are overrepresented; informal learning, Global South practice, disability-led scholarship, vocational settings, procurement, cybersecurity, and day-to-day commercial product work are under-sampled. Paywalls constrained passage-level inspection for some DOI records. Publication frequency was never treated as prevalence of practice.

Stopping was pragmatic rather than saturation in the systematic-review sense: the run stopped when each emerging cluster had (a) work evidence or a normative basis, (b) at least one adjacent or failure-oriented counter-search, and (c) enough differentiation to classify core, literacy, specialist, stewardship, or team responsibility. New searches were still yielding examples, but not new top-level work functions. The source base and all claims remain open to correction.

## 4. Candidate capability architecture

The domains below were clustered **after** extracting work functions. They are a lifecycle, not departments, and none implies that one person owns the whole domain.

1. **Problem, context, and system inquiry.** Organizes diagnosis before solution selection. Core: distinguish kinds of problem and make assumptions inspectable. Team: include local/learner knowledge. Boundary: how much organizational-change diagnosis belongs to a practitioner versus leadership?
2. **Learning, task, and evidence modeling.** Organizes explicit claims about what changes and what observations warrant conclusions. Core: maintain alignment literacy. Specialist: cognitive task analysis and psychometrics. Boundary: simple formative decisions need less formal modeling than high-stakes decisions.
3. **Human-centered learning intervention design.** Organizes translation of theory into usable practice, feedback, and interaction. Core: coherent rationale and formative testing. Specialist/team: interaction design, content expertise, disability expertise. Boundary: evidence supports principles unevenly across populations and subjects.
4. **Technical realization and observability.** Organizes building, testing, instrumenting, and maintaining artifacts. Collaborative literacy: specify and inspect behavior/data. Specialist: production engineering, architecture, security, ML operations. Boundary: non-digital programs still require operational fidelity and records but not software skill.
5. **Inference, evaluation, and disciplined improvement.** Organizes learning from variation without overstating claims. Core: question/measure fit and uncertainty. Specialist: causal inference, psychometrics, complex statistics. Team: mixed-method evaluation. Boundary: improvement evidence and summative causal evidence answer different questions.
6. **Implementation and organizational embedding.** Organizes fit, adoption, adaptation, scale, cost, and sustainment. Core: anticipate context and document adaptations. Leadership/team: resources, incentives, ownership, procurement, change management. Boundary: health implementation frameworks transfer conceptually, but educational validation is incomplete.
7. **Rights, inclusion, safety, and stewardship.** Organizes accessibility, privacy, fairness, agency, and AI risk as lifecycle constraints. Common ethical obligation: identify and escalate foreseeable risks. Specialists: privacy/security/legal/accessibility/algorithm audit. Leadership: risk acceptance and remedy. Boundary: compliance is a floor, and fairness metrics encode incompatible values.
8. **Boundary-spanning collaboration and knowledge stewardship.** Organizes shared understanding, decision rights, traceable rationales, and reusable learning. Collaborative literacy is universal; facilitation and portfolio stewardship may be specialist/leadership capabilities. Boundary: “communication skill” is too vague unless evidenced by artifacts and consequential coordination.

### Evidence/inference/proposal trace example

To keep the three levels visible: **reported evidence**—Dynarski et al. report that evaluated reading and mathematics software products did not significantly raise test scores on average in the first study year, alongside implementation/usage information. **Capability inference**—teams must distinguish availability and use from learning effect and investigate implementation, measure, mechanism, and context after null results. **Competency proposal**—LE-X09 specifies an observable rival-explanation analysis and a documented revise/stop decision. The report does *not* establish that possessing LE-X09 caused better outcomes.

## 6. Weak signals and possible omissions

- **Procurement and vendor-claim due diligence.** Consequential in institutions, especially for AI, but this run found standards/risk guidance rather than enough authentic procurement work to formulate performance criteria.
- **Learning-content supply-chain and intellectual-property stewardship.** Rights, provenance, licensing, and culturally responsible reuse appear important; direct outcome-connected learning-engineering evidence was thin.
- **Environmental sustainability.** Compute, device, and replacement costs are plausible lifecycle concerns, but evidence connecting an observable learning-engineering capability to outcomes was insufficient.
- **Cybersecurity incident readiness.** Clearly required at team level for learner systems, but specialist security frameworks would overwhelm the learning-specific evidence sampled here; retain as a handoff/escalation obligation pending targeted study.
- **Teacher/instructor orchestration and professional learning.** Implementation sources imply it, yet the present sample did not adequately distinguish designing professional learning from broader implementation competence.
- **Learner co-governance and collective data rights.** Ethics literature makes the normative case for agency and power analysis, but assessable practices and cross-sector applicability require disability-led, Indigenous-data-governance, and student-partnership sources.
- **Economic evaluation and opportunity-cost reasoning.** Cost is an implementation outcome, but robust educational examples and responsibility boundaries were under-sampled.
- **Futures/anticipatory governance.** Scenario testing may matter for fast-changing AI systems; current evidence is chiefly normative and generic.

## 7. Contradictions and unresolved questions

1. **A profession or a team configuration?** Sources document overlapping design, research, analysis, engineering, and implementation work but do not establish a single occupation. A unified framework could erase specialist accountability.
2. **Research versus improvement.** Experimental standards prioritize identification and pre-specification; improvement/DBR prioritize local iteration and adaptive learning. Both are useful, but claims must not migrate from one evidentiary regime to the other.
3. **Fidelity versus adaptation.** Replication demands stable core functions; fit and equity may demand modification. Evidence does not provide a universal boundary between acceptable and mechanism-breaking adaptation.
4. **General principles versus domain expertise.** Retrieval practice and feedback findings offer design resources, not recipes. Target knowledge, learner age, culture, and setting condition performance; deep subject-matter expertise may reside elsewhere on the team.
5. **Measurement utility versus surveillance.** More instrumentation can improve diagnosis while increasing privacy, chilling, proxy, and power risks. “Collect everything” is technically convenient but professionally indefensible.
6. **Personalization versus fairness/agency.** Aggregate accuracy can coexist with subgroup error and constrained opportunity. No single fairness statistic resolves educational values; governance must expose the choice and provide remedy.
7. **Accessibility standard versus lived usability.** WCAG offers testable minimums; conformance does not prove usable or equitable learning, and automated checks cannot replace disabled-user participation.
8. **Engagement versus learning.** Time-on-task, clicks, completion, and satisfaction can be useful process measures, but cannot silently substitute for retention, transfer, or intended capability.
9. **Rapid tests versus long-run outcomes.** Product experiments favor speed; education may require delayed transfer, cumulative effects, and protection from opportunity costs. Short cycles need balancing and longer-horizon measures.
10. **Ethics as individual competence versus institutional power.** An individual can identify and escalate risk but may lack authority to stop deployment, fund accessibility, or grant remedy. Frameworks should assess leadership and governance structures, not transfer all liability downward.
11. **Sector variation.** High-stakes assessment needs stronger psychometrics and legal safeguards; classroom improvement needs feasibility and teacher orchestration; consumer products emphasize software operations and experimentation; workforce systems emphasize job/task transfer. A single flat list would mislead.

## 8. Provisional conclusions and next searches

### Strongest candidates for validation

The most defensible common-core candidates are problem/context diagnosis (LE-X01), explicit theory/assumption modeling (LE-X02), aligned design (LE-X04), data/measure skepticism (LE-X07), warranted interpretation of evaluation (LE-X08), disciplined response to null/mixed results (LE-X09), implementation fit (LE-X11), and ethical-risk recognition/escalation (the core portions of LE-X14/15). Strong team capabilities are valid measurement (LE-X03), accessible participatory design (LE-X05), reliable instrumentation (LE-X06), evaluation execution (LE-X08), adaptation/sustainment (LE-X12/13), and risk governance (LE-X14/15).

“Strongest” here means **best supported within this bounded run**, not a ranking of practitioner importance or proof of universality.

### Practitioner review and interviews needed

Use critical-incident interviews across K–12 districts, universities, workplace learning, assessment vendors, and consumer learning products. Ask for the last consequential design decision, the artifact used, what inadequate performance looked like, which role held authority, what was escalated, and what happened after a null or harmful result. Specifically test whether LE-X02, X06, X10, X13, and X16 are observable in normal work; whether core literacy thresholds are realistic; and who can actually stop deployment.

### Targeted follow-up searches

1. Authentic job analyses and durable postings sampled by sector, seniority, and geography—not convenience examples.
2. Education-specific implementation and sustainment cases, including failed scale-up and total-cost evidence.
3. Disability-led, multilingual, Indigenous, and Global South design/governance evidence.
4. Learning-platform data incidents, invalid predictive alerts, subgroup harms, and documented remediation.
5. Procurement/RFP language and vendor evidence review for adaptive and generative-AI systems.
6. Comparative cases where engagement rose but retention/transfer did not, and where teams changed course after null results.
7. Boundary studies of psychometric, causal, software, privacy, accessibility, and facilitation specialist handoffs.
8. Non-digital, vocational, informal, early-childhood, and adult/basic-education work functions.

### Questions to preserve for later IEEE comparison

- Does the later framework separate observable performance from knowledge, values, tools, job titles, and fields?
- Does it distinguish core competence, collaborative literacy, specialist execution, leadership authority, and team infrastructure?
- Are implementation, sustainment, decommissioning, accessibility, privacy, power, redress, and null-result learning explicit?
- Do competencies specify artifacts, quality criteria, constraints, and warranted claim scope?
- Are sector profiles and proficiency thresholds supported, or merely assumed universal?
- Can evidence and disagreement be updated without preserving the present clusters?

These questions are deliberately preserved without consulting an existing IEEE competency framework; comparison belongs to a later, separate phase.
